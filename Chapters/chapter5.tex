\chapter{Ant Colony System by Dorigo et al}\label{chapt:5}



\section{Fundamentals}

\subsection{Biological Inspiration}
The Ant Colony System (\Gls{ACS}) draws inspiration from real ants, which can efficiently find the shortest paths between food sources and their nest. This ability is attributed to the pheromone trails laid down by ants, facilitating an indirect form of communication that guides other ants towards optimal paths\cite{Dorigo1996, Dorigo1997}.

Stigmergy, a mechanism of indirect coordination between agents, plays a crucial role in this process\cite{Bonabeau1999}. Ants deposit pheromones as they move, creating positive feedback that reinforces the most efficient paths over time\cite{Deneubourg1990}.

\subsection{Algorithmic Principles}
The \Gls{ACS} leverages a colony of artificial ants that work cooperatively to explore and exploit solutions to optimization problems. The approach integrates both the exploration of new solutions and the exploitation of known good solutions through a balance between pheromone influence and heuristic desirability.\cite{Dorigo1996, Dorigo1997}

Dorigo et al. introduced several improvements over previous ant-based algorithms:

\begin{itemize}
	\item A stronger state transition rule that better balances the exploration of new edges and the exploitation of accumulated knowledge about the problem.
	\item A global pheromone update rule applied only to edges belonging to the best tour.
	\item The application of a local pheromone update rule during solution construction.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Application to \Gls{TSP}}
The \Gls{TSP} is particularly well-suited for the application of \Gls{ACS} due to its need for efficient path-finding solutions. The algorithm simulates ant behavior to construct paths, with the optimization process guided by both heuristic information (e.g., distances between cities) and pheromone trails that represent a form of learned experience\cite{Dorigo1997, Gambardella1995}.

In the context of the \Gls{TSP}, each ant constructs a tour by visiting all cities exactly once. The choice of the next city to visit is influenced by both the distance (heuristic information) and the amount of pheromones on the edge connecting the cities (accumulated experience).

\section{Implementation Details}

\subsection{Pheromone Update Rules}

The pheromone update rules in \Gls{ACS} are crucial for guiding the search process towards promising areas of the solution space. These rules are divided into local and global updates.

\subsubsection{Local Update Rule}
Each time an ant traverses an edge $(i,j)$, it applies the local pheromone update rule to decrease the pheromone level, encouraging exploration by other ants. The local update rule is given by:
\[
	\tau_{ij} = (1 - \rho) \cdot \tau_{ij} + \rho \cdot \tau_0
\]
where $\tau_{ij}$ is the pheromone concentration on edge $(i,j)$, $\rho$ is the local evaporation rate (0 < $\rho$ < 1) and $\tau_0$ is the initial pheromone level.

The effect of this rule is to reduce the amount of pheromone on a visited edge, making that edge less desirable for subsequent ants. This increases the exploration of alternative paths and helps prevent premature stagnation on suboptimal solutions.

\subsubsection{Global Update Rule}
After all ants have completed their tours, the global update rule is applied to edges that belong to the best tour (either globally or in the current iteration). The global update enhances the pheromone trail on critical paths, making them more attractive for future ants. The global update rule is defined as:
\[
	\tau_{ij} = (1 - \alpha) \cdot \tau_{ij} + \alpha \cdot \Delta \tau_{ij}^{\text{best}}
\]
where $\alpha$ is the global evaporation rate (0 < $\alpha$ < 1) and $\Delta \tau_{ij}^{\text{best}}$ represents the pheromones deposited by the best ant, defined as:
\[
	\Delta \tau_{ij}^{\text{best}} = \begin{cases}
		(L_{\text{best}})^{-1} & \text{if }(i,j)\text{ belongs to the best tour} \\
		0                      & \text{otherwise}
	\end{cases}
\]
where $L_{\text{best}}$ is the length of the best tour.

This rule intensifies pheromones on paths that have led to the best solutions, guiding the search towards promising regions of the solution space.

\subsection{Ant Movement Rules}

Ants select the next city to visit based on a probabilistic decision rule that balances the exploration of new paths with the exploitation of pheromone trails and heuristic information.

\subsubsection{Transition Rule}
In \Gls{ACS} the choice of the next city to visit is governed by the pseudo-random-proportional transition rule. Given an ant $k$ in city $i$, the probability of moving to city $j$ is determined by:

\[
	j = \begin{cases}
		\arg\max_{l \in N_i^k} \{[\tau_{il}] \cdot [\eta_{il}]^\beta\} & \text{if }q \leq q_0 \text{ (exploitation)} \\
		J                                                              & \text{otherwise (balanced exploration)}
	\end{cases}
\]

where $q$ is a random number uniformly distributed in $[0,1]$, $q_0$ is a parameter ($0 \le q_0 \le 1$) that determines the balance between exploitation and exploration, $\tau_{il}$ is the amount of pheromone on edge $(i,l)$, $\eta_{il}$ is the heuristic value (usually the inverse of the distance), $\beta$ is a parameter that controls the relative importance of heuristic information, and $N_i^k$ is the set of cities not yet visited by ant $k$ positioned in city $i$.

$J$ is a random city chosen according to the probability distribution:

\[
	p_{ij}^k = \frac{[\tau_{ij}] \cdot [\eta_{ij}]^\beta}{\sum_{l \in N_i^k} [\tau_{il}] \cdot [\eta_{il}]^\beta}
\]

This transition rule favors the exploitation of accumulated information (when $q \leq q_0$) but leaves room for exploration (when $q > q_0$).

\subsection{Pseudocode}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
	\floatname{ACS}{}
	\caption{\Gls{ACS} for the \Gls{TSP}}
	\begin{algorithmic}[1]
		\State Initialize pheromone levels $\tau_{ij} = \tau_0$ for all edges $(i,j)$
		\State Initialize the best global solution $S_{gb}$ and its length $L_{gb}$
		\For{each iteration}
		\For{each ant $k = 1, \ldots, m$}
		\State Place ant $k$ on a random starting city
		\State Initialize partial tour $S_k = \emptyset$
		\Repeat
		\State Select the next city $j$ using the transition rule
		\State Add $(i,j)$ to $S_k$
		\State Apply local pheromone update to $(i,j)$
		\State $i \gets j$
		\Until{tour $S_k$ is complete}
		\If{$L(S_k) < L_{gb}$}
		\State $S_{gb} \gets S_k$
		\State $L_{gb} \gets L(S_k)$
		\EndIf
		\EndFor
		\State Apply global pheromone update to the best tour $S_{gb}$
		\EndFor
		\State \Return the best solution found $S_{gb}$
	\end{algorithmic}
	\label{alg:acs}
\end{algorithm}



\subsection{Complexity Analysis}
The time complexity of \Gls{ACS} per iteration is O($n^2m$), where $n$ is the number of cities and $m$ is the number of ants. However, the number of iterations required for convergence can vary significantly depending on problem characteristics and algorithm parameters.

\subsection{Key Parameters and their Impact}
The effectiveness of \Gls{ACS} crucially depends on the proper setting of several parameters:

\begin{itemize}
	\item $\rho$: Local pheromone evaporation rate
	\item $\alpha$: Global pheromone evaporation rate
	\item $\beta$: Relative importance of heuristic information
	\item $q_0$: Balance between exploitation and exploration
	\item $m$: Number of ants
	\item $\tau_0$: Initial pheromone level
\end{itemize}

The optimal choice of these parameters can vary depending on the specific problem and often requires a tuning phase.

\section{Variants and Extensions}

Since the original publication of \Gls{ACS}, numerous variants and extensions have been proposed:
\begin{itemize}
	\item MAX-MIN Ant System (\Gls{MMAS}): Introduced by Stützle and Hoos, \Gls{MMAS} limits pheromone values within a predefined range to prevent premature stagnation. \cite{Stuetzle1997}
	\item Rank-Based Ant System (\Gls{ASrank}): In this variant, ants are ranked according to the quality of their solutions, and the amount of pheromones deposited is proportional to their rank.
	\item Hybrid ACS: Combinations of ACS with other optimization techniques, such as genetic algorithms or Tabu Search, have shown promising results on various combinatorial optimization problems.
\end{itemize}

\section{Applications beyond the \Gls{TSP}}

Although initially developed for the \Gls{TSP}, \Gls{ACS} and its variants have found applications in a wide range of domains:

\begin{itemize}
	\item Vehicle routing problems
	\item Scheduling and planning
	\item Frequency assignment in telecommunication networks
	\item Bioinformatics and protein folding
	\item Network optimization and grid computing
	\item Multi-objective optimization problems
\end{itemize}

The versatility of \Gls{ACS} makes it a powerful approach for tackling complex optimization problems in various fields of engineering and science.
